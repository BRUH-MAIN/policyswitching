`report_content/figures/presentation/results_tables.md` (round 1) or
`report_content/figures/presentation_r2/results_tables.md` (round 2), generated by

## The question and why the comparison is built this way

The project trained terrain-specialist policies (flat, rough, stairs) and switches between them
while the robot follows a person. The question for this presentation is whether switching between
specialists works better than a single generalist policy.

The earlier study (2026-10-05, `final_report.md` Section V.H) found a 55-78 point lead for
switching over the old generalists. However, it also found that switching scored no better than the
best single specialist, so the lead came from better training, not from switching. Since then the
stairs specialist went through seven more versions (v2 to v8b, `fig10_project_arc`), and no
generalist had any of that work. A comparison against the old generalist v2 would credit switching
with a training gap.

So the generalist here is **matched**. GeneralistV3 is stairs v8b's exact training recipe (rewards,
curriculum, 0-30 ms actuator-delay randomisation, observations), applied to flat + rough + stairs
at equal weight. It is warm-started from v8b's final checkpoint and trained 4,000 more iterations
(job 12816). It has the specialist's lineage, more training than the specialist, and differs only
in terrain breadth (`fig11_training_curves`: on stairs it plateaus at the same rows as v8b, 6.39 up
and 5.99 down against 6.14 and 6.11).

## Test

- **Layouts.** 24 randomised courses (seeds 1000-1023). Each has a flat stretch, rough ground, an
  up-staircase and a down-staircase in random order. The flights are 10 steps, at 9 / 12 / 15 /
  17 cm risers with six layouts each. Leader speed is 0.4-0.7 m/s, and the robot follows 3 m
  behind. Sensor noise is on, with 256 trials per layout per arm.
- **Arms.** All three controllers switch between the same bank (flat, rough, stairs v8b):
  - **A, oracle switch:** hard switch 0.8 m before each terrain edge, from the course layout. 0.8 m
    is the edge of the robot's own height scan.
  - **B, onboard-scan switch:** a small classifier on the robot's own height scan picks the
    specialist. It is retrained on separate layouts (2000-2011) because the old one only saw 5-7 cm
    risers.
  - **C, soft blend:** a linear cross-fade from 0.8 m before the edge to the edge.
- **Baselines:** the matched generalist, stairs v8b alone, and the old generalist v2 for reference.
- **Fall definitions.**
  - Primary: **tipping only**, which is v8b's training definition and the robot acceptance
    criterion.
  - Secondary: **strict**, where any shin, knee or body contact with the ground counts as a fall.
  - Robustness: every arm again with a 20 ms motor delay.
- **Statistics.** The layout is the unit of analysis: means over layouts with 95% bootstrap
  intervals over layouts, and paired per-layout differences.

## Round 1 results (Addendum 8)

### 1. Primary result: with the pre-registered measure, switching and the matched generalist tie

| arm | course success, tipping only (95% CI) |
|---|---|
| A oracle switch | **99.4%** (99.1-99.7) |
| B onboard-scan switch | **85.0%** (73.8-95.0) |
| C soft blend | **99.5%** (99.1-99.8) |
| matched generalist V3 | **99.2%** (98.7-99.6) |
| stairs v8b alone | **99.8%** (99.5-100.0) |
| generalist v2 (old) | 23.2% (7.7-42.1) |

The pre-registered primary claims, as paired per-layout differences against the matched generalist:

- **A:** +0.3 points (-0.1 to +0.6), ahead in 9 layouts and behind in 7.
- **C:** +0.3 (+0.0 to +0.6), 11 / 3.
- **B:** -14.2 (-24.9 to -4.6).

A and C are not meaningfully better than the generalist. Every policy trained with the v8b recipe
sits at about 99%, which is a ceiling. Against stairs v8b alone, both A and C are slightly
*worse*: -0.4 (-0.7 to -0.1) and -0.3 (-0.5 to -0.1). The old generalist v2's 23% shows how
large the training gap was (it crosses 0% of courses with risers of 12 cm or more).

### 2. Secondary result: by the strict contact measure, oracle switching beats the generalist clearly

When any shin, knee or body contact counts as a failure:

| arm | strict success (95% CI) |
|---|---|
| A oracle switch | **50.0%** (41.7-58.0) |
| B onboard-scan switch | 14.0% (8.2-20.4) |
| C soft blend | **38.9%** (32.1-45.8) |
| matched generalist V3 | 19.7% (15.4-24.1) |
| stairs v8b alone | 45.9% (38.4-53.3) |

The paired differences:

- **A minus the generalist:** **+30.3 points (+23.2 to +37.6), ahead in all 24 layouts.**
- **C minus the generalist:** +19.2 (+12.3 to +26.6), ahead in 21.
- **A minus v8b alone:** +4.0 (+0.3 to +7.8).

The generalist pays for its breadth in foot placement. Most of its strict failures are on
down-flights (58% of trials, against 37.5% for A and 42.9% for v8b alone, `fig05`). It still gets
down without tipping, but it brushes the steps far more often. This is the one place where
switching wins, and by a wide margin. It is a secondary, pre-registered measure, not the primary
one.

### 3. With the robot's own sensor (arm B), switching is worse, and the reason is specific

B matches A at 9-15 cm (99.4-99.9%) and collapses at 17 cm (40.8%). In 44.7% of 17 cm trials the
classifier never selects "stairs" before the robot stops. Those robots stop 0.2-0.46 m before the
first up-step, still on flat ground, and the leader walks out of range (`fig08`).

The mechanism is in the labels. The classifier learns the "footprint" label, which only changes to
stairs about 0.3 m before the edge. A flat specialist will not walk that close to a 17 cm riser, so
the robot stalls before the label, and so the classifier, ever says "stairs". The oracle switches
at 0.8 m and never meets this.

The classifier itself is accurate where it matters: 99.5% recall on stairs and 89.9% overall on
held-out trials. Its weak spot is rough ground read as flat (71% rough recall). That swap is
harmless here, but it makes B switch 30 times on the demo course against the oracle's 12.

Training the classifier on labels that lead the edge by 0.8 m is the obvious fix. It was not
pre-registered or tested.

### 4. Under a 20 ms motor delay, the switching arms lose about 6 points

The losses are for A (99.4 to 93.8) and C (99.5 to 93.5). The generalist (99.2 to 98.5) and v8b
alone (99.8 to 99.8) barely move. Against the generalist, A is -4.7 (-6.3 to -3.0) and C is -5.0.
The cause is again training: the flat and rough specialists in the bank predate the delay
randomisation that v8b and the generalist were trained with.

### 5. Motion quality

Median action rate entering terrain changes (`fig06`) is 2.09 for A, 1.95 for C, 1.98 for the
generalist and 2.07 for v8b alone. The soft blend gives the smoothest entries, but the spread is
small. Following-gap error is 0.15-0.18 m for all v8b-recipe arms, against 0.37 m for generalist
v2. Time to finish is the same for all of them (about 34 s).

## Qualitative

The same 15 cm course was recorded with each policy (`report_content/videos/presentation/`,
stills in `figures/presentation/stills/`). All four v8b-recipe policies finish it, and the climbs
look alike. The visible difference is in the switching itself:

- The oracle switches 12 times, at the terrain edges.
- The onboard classifier switches 30 times, flickering between flat and rough on rough ground. This
  is visible in the bottom strip of `classifier_end.png` and doesn't cause a fall on this course.

## Round 2: switching with its handicaps removed (Addendum 9)

Round 1 left switching with two handicaps, both training or engineering and not the idea itself:
the bank's flat and rough specialists predated the v8b recipe, and the onboard classifier learned
to switch too late. Round 2 removed both and kept the comparison fair:

- **Bank R.** New flat and rough specialists with exactly the generalist's recipe (its config cut
  to one terrain class), each warm-started from GeneralistV3 and trained 2,000 iterations. The
  stairs slot is still v8b.
- **Compute-matched generalist.** GeneralistV3 trained 2,000 more iterations, to 6,000
  (`fig11_training_curves_r2`; it ends at up-flight row 6.40 and down-flight row 6.09).
- **Classifier.** It is retrained on labels that turn to "stairs" 0.8 m before the edge, the
  oracle's lead. Its filter hold (10 steps) was picked from {5, 10, 20} on the training layouts
  only. Held-out accuracy is 89.1%, with stairs recall 99.0%.
- **Primary measure.** Strict success (any non-foot contact is a fall) is now primary, declared
  before any round-2 data. Round 1 showed tipping-only success at a ceiling, and on the robot a
  shin striking a nosing is a real failure.
- **Fresh test layouts** (seeds 1100-1123), same design otherwise.

### Primary result: all three switching controllers beat the generalist by 25-30 points

| arm | strict success (95% CI) | minus matched generalist | layouts ahead / behind |
|---|---|---|---|
| A oracle switch | **44.6%** (37.9-51.1) | **+29.7** (+22.8 to +36.4) | 23 / 1 |
| B onboard-scan switch | **44.7%** (37.9-51.4) | **+29.8** (+23.0 to +36.5) | 22 / 1 |
| C soft blend | **39.6%** (33.9-45.0) | **+24.7** (+19.0 to +30.0) | 22 / 2 |
| matched generalist, 6,000 it | 14.9% (11.2-18.7) | | |
| stairs v8b alone | 44.4% (37.5-51.1) | | |
| A with the old bank | 44.3% (37.9-50.9) | | |

All three pre-registered primary claims hold, on layouts never used before. Round 1's +30 for the
oracle switch replicates (+29.7). The generalist's extra failures are again on stairs: 50.3% of
its trials end on a down-flight and 16.2% on an up-flight, against 39.3% and 8.0% for the oracle
switch.

### The onboard switch now matches the oracle

Arm B scores 44.7% strict against A's 44.6%. Tipping-only it is 99.0% at 17 cm, against 40.8% in
round 1. It now picks "stairs" 0.6-0.7 m before the first step (round 1: 0.1-0.3 m), and it fails
to pick it before stopping in 0.2% of 17 cm trials (round 1: 44.7%) (`fig12_classifier_before_after`).
So the robot gets switching's advantage from its own height scan, with no map and no leader preview.

### Secondary results

- **Against stairs v8b alone, switching is a tie.** A is +0.2 (-1.4 to +2.0), B +0.4 (-1.6 to +2.3),
  and C -4.8 (-7.9 to -2.0). On these courses the stairs specialist also handles flat and rough
  ground well, so the gain over the generalist comes from the specialist's training. Switching is
  how a robot gets to use a specialist safely on mixed terrain; it adds nothing on top of the best
  single specialist here. The soft blend's deficit is on flat ground (14.0% of its trials end there
  against 5.6% for A): blending two policies' actions makes contacts neither policy makes alone.
- **Tipping-only.** Every arm is at 99.2-99.7%, and A minus the generalist is +0.3 (+0.0 to +0.6).
- **20 ms motor delay, strict.** A 31.7%, B 31.1%, C 27.0%, generalist 14.0%, v8b alone 34.0%,
  and A with the old bank 26.4%. Switching still beats the generalist (A +17.7, +11.6 to +24.2,
  22 of 24 layouts). The retrained bank recovers 5 points under delay (26.4 to 31.7), but switching
  remains 2-3 points behind v8b alone (A -2.2, -3.8 to -0.7).
- **Retraining the bank** changed nothing without delay (44.6% vs 44.3%), only under delay.

## What to conclude (both rounds)

1. **A switched specialist bank beats a matched generalist on clean, contact-free stair walking.**
   It scores +30 points by the strict measure, replicated across two pre-registered rounds and 48
   layouts. In round 2 that is the primary measure, the generalist has the same recipe and the
   same compute, and the switch is made from the robot's own height scan.
2. **On getting across without tipping, they tie at about 99%.** That measure is at its ceiling.
3. **The advantage is the specialist's.** Stairs v8b alone ties with switching on these courses.
   The honest claim is "specialists beat a generalist, and switching lets the robot use them". It is
   not "switching beats the best single specialist".
4. **Specialist training was the largest effect in the project.** It took the stairs policy from
   crossing 0% of 17 cm flights (stairs v2, 5-step flights) to 100% of 17 cm, 10-step flights (v8b,
   `fig10`).
5. **How the switch is timed matters.** A classifier imitating where the robot is switches too
   late for tall risers. Labels that lead the edge by the oracle's 0.8 m fix it completely.
6. **The soft blend is the weakest controller,** and the best single policy is the most robust to
   motor delay.

## Limitations

- **Simulation only.** No result here was measured on the real robot.
- **One training seed per policy.** The intervals cover layout variation, not training variation.
- **Courses too easy to show switching beating the best specialist.** On these courses the stairs
  specialist also walks flat and rough ground well, so they cannot show a case where switching
  beats the best single specialist. Terrains whose skills conflict (gaps, slopes, taller stairs)
  would be the test; not run.
- **Round 1's primary measure was at its ceiling.** At about 99% for every v8b-recipe policy,
  tipping-only success cannot separate the arms. Round 2 made strict success primary, declared
  before its data.
- **Classifier training data.** It was trained on simulated scans with the simulator's noise model.
  A real elevation map has other faults (latency, dropouts, drift).
